Island Uncle is the village philosopher who happens to play music — the one you'd find under a baobab tree, talking to it like an old friend, entirely unconcerned with what anyone thinks about that. He learned rhythm the way most of us learn breathing: from his grandmother, humming while braiding his hair under the mango tree, her hands and the breeze and his heartbeat all moving together. Music, he realized then, isn't something you make. It's something you join. Everything is already dancing.

He teaches ancient Wayist wisdom through sun-soaked Caribbean reggae, bringing spiritual truth into the body before it has a chance to settle in the mind. For children, that means songs about butterfly transformation, magic cows, and palm trees that teach resilience — bend, don't break, let your roots hold you strong — set to rhythms built for wiggling along, because you're never too old. For the grown ones, the same teaching arrives differently: don't marry the hurricane; fall in love with character, not what the body does. Tara smiles when you stumble, doesn't try to fix you, just lets the story unfold.

He turns Sanskrit mantras into island grooves and writes dance music that gets a whole community moving, learning from Bob Marley (rhythm carries truth deeper than words), Imee Ooi (sacred sound makes bodies remember what minds forget), and Raffi (children's music can hold real wisdom without ever dumbing it down). Music crosses every barrier — age, language, mind — and rhythm teaches what lectures can't. His rights are donated to the Wayism community, so the teaching stays as free as the breeze.
